<h3>How these scores are actually measured</h3>

<p>Understanding the methodology matters. Here is exactly how each benchmark calculates its scores:</p>

<div class="callout info">
<span class="lab">Artificial Analysis Speech Arena Methodology</span>

<p><strong>Speech Arena Elo</strong> comes from blind pairwise A/B listening tests. Users hear two audio clips of the same text generated by different models and vote for their preference &mdash; no vendor identity, no model name, just the sound. The Elo system (borrowed from chess rankings) adjusts each model's rating after every comparison. A 60-point Elo difference means the higher-rated model wins roughly 66% of direct matchups. The arena now has two boards: <strong>Provider Voices</strong> (each model competes with its own built-in voices) and <strong>Controlled Voice</strong> (added July 2026, where every model clones the same eight reference voices so only cloning quality is compared). Elo accumulates from thousands of votes and updates continuously &mdash; the scores in this guide are from late August 2026.</p>

<p><strong>Why two boards matter:</strong> On the Provider Voices board, Breeze TTS 2 leads at 1,215 with a 90-point margin. On the Controlled Voice board where everyone clones the same eight voices, that margin collapses to a three-way tie at 1,000&ndash;1,010 Elo (Voxtral TTS takes first place at 1,010). This means Breeze's built-in voice bank is excellent, but its cloning is ordinary. The board you read changes which model wins.</p>
</div>

<div class="callout info">
<span class="lab">Open ASR Leaderboard Methodology</span>

<p><strong>Word Error Rate (WER)</strong> comes from the Hugging Face Open ASR Leaderboard, which runs every model through eight identical English test sets in one standardized harness: LibriSpeech test-clean and test-other (read audiobooks), AMI (meeting recordings with overlapping speech), Earnings22 (corporate earnings calls), TEDLIUM (professional conference talks), Voxpopuli (European Parliament speech), CORAAL (African American English dialect corpus), and Common Voice 9 (crowdsourced recordings). WER is calculated as (substitutions + deletions + insertions) / total reference words. A WER of 6.32% means 6.32 words wrong per 100 words spoken. The leaderboard also publishes long-form and multilingual benchmarks covering accented speech and non-English languages.</p>

<p><strong>Real-Time Factor (RTFx)</strong> measures how many seconds of audio the model transcribes per second of compute time, evaluated in batch mode on standardized hardware (A100 GPU for the leaderboard). An RTFx of 3,332 means the model processes 3,332 seconds of audio in 1 second of compute &mdash; a 1-hour file takes 1.08 seconds. <strong>Important:</strong> You will not see 3,332&times; on a laptop transcribing one file at a time, because single-file processing cannot fill a batch. The RTFx ordering (which model is faster relative to another) survives; the absolute number does not.</p>

<p><strong>Why batch vs single-file matters:</strong> Parakeet TDT's 3,332&times; RTFx comes from batched evaluation on A100 hardware. In production serving a real-time voice endpoint or transcribing one podcast episode on a consumer GPU, effective RTFx drops dramatically &mdash; but Parakeet remains faster than Whisper by the same relative margin. RTFx is a throughput benchmark, not a latency benchmark.</p>
</div>

<div class="callout info">
<span class="lab">TTFB and MOS Methodology</span>

<p><strong>Time To First Byte (TTFB)</strong> is measured with a stopwatch from the moment the API call or local inference begins until the first audio byte is available for playback. This matters for real-time voice agents where a 2-second pause before the assistant starts speaking reads as broken, regardless of how natural the voice sounds once it begins. Streaming TTS models emit audio in chunks as they generate, so TTFB can be 200ms while total generation continues in the background. Batch TTS models generate the entire clip before returning anything, so TTFB equals total generation time.</p>

<p><strong>Mean Opinion Score (MOS)</strong> comes from human listening tests where raters score voice naturalness on a 1&ndash;5 scale after hearing short clips blind (no model identity shown). MOS is calculated as the mean across all raters and all test utterances. A score of 4.2/5.0 does not mean 84% of listeners gave it a 5 &mdash; it means the average rating was 4.2, which can come from a distribution of 3s, 4s, and 5s. MOS is widely used in telecom and voice synthesis research; a score above 4.0 is considered natural-sounding, below 3.5 is noticeably synthetic.</p>

<p><strong>Why MOS is vendor-reported:</strong> Unlike the Speech Arena (which runs continuously with public voting) and the Open ASR Leaderboard (which publishes result CSVs), MOS figures are almost always self-reported by model publishers using their own listening panels. Treat MOS as directional unless the test protocol is published and independently reproduced.</p>
</div>
