October 2026 AI Model Updates: Every Launch, Access Change, and Price Move

TL;DR — October 2026 opened with Gemini 4 Argon reaching general availability (October 13) at $2/$10 introductory pricing that doubles December 1, Meta Muse Wonder launching (October 12) as Meta's first closed frontier model at $4/$20, and Anthropic shipping Claude Haiku 5.5 (October 7) — the fastest Claude with 1M context, 128K output, and adaptive thinking at $0.10/$0.50 per million tokens, positioned 75-90% cheaper than Haiku 4.5. Earlier: Mistral previewed Large 4 (October 6) — a 1-trillion-parameter multimodal mixture of experts behind a guardrailed Studio preview — and the month's first three days produced eight specialist models including decision models from Cloudflare and Amazon, a voice stack from Microsoft, and Bilibili's open-weight translator. OpenAI shipped no GPT-6.1 Astra; the Wall Street Journal reported the release was cancelled after internal safety tests. What shipped in late September: Claude Sonnet 5.5 (September 28) scored 56.0 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at $2/$10, and DevDay's GPT-6.1 Sol (September 29) scored 51.8 at $0.72 per task — the cheapest run in the top tier. Cost per completed task, not cost per token, now decides routing. Mid-month the quiet broke in price rather than in capability: DeepSeek shipped V4.1-Flash-1017 (October 17) — every official benchmark up, outputs shorter, token prices unchanged, Search and Fetch APIs opened at $0.75 per 1,000 calls with free harness calls until October 31, and 552B MIT weights on Hugging Face the same day — and Tencent answered the memory problem with Hy Memory 2.0 (October 16), an open model, framework and MemHub built around the Hy-Memory-30B-A3B writer. The final week delivered the month's third act from the open-weight side: MiniMax launched M3.1 Flash (October 19) — a 428B/23B multimodal MoE at $0.15/$0.60 per million tokens with an Artificial Analysis index reading of 43 at $0.22 per task — and published the weights on Hugging Face October 23; and Moonshot shipped Kimi K3.1 Pro (October 23), the first model in its K3.1 series: 2.8 trillion parameters with 104 billion active, a 1M-token context, $2/$10 pricing, Terminal-Bench 4.0 at 65.7%, and an Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index score of 55 — the highest open-weight reading ever recorded and fourth place overall, with full weights due November 4. Google also pushed Gemini 4 Argon into AI Mode in Search across 19 countries (October 19). And on October 27 the month grew a fifth act from a different part of the forest: Liquid AI shipped LFM3 — six 2B-class open-weight models (text, vision, audio, omni, and two decision models) branched from a single tri-hybrid LFM3-1.7B-Base backbone trained on 22 trillion tokens. The text model averages 49.37 across its reported benchmark suite against 47.24 for Qwen3.5-9B with roughly one-fifth the parameters, carries a 256K-token context, and runs in about 1 GB of memory at 4-bit — the strongest signal yet that the frontier's most consequential competition is moving to the edge. The day before, the closed side of the fence answered the Astra-shaped hole in its calendar from below rather than above: GPT-6.1 Terra (announced October 26) is a $1/$5 mid-tier that matches GPT-6 Astra on computer-use benchmarks at a tenth of the token price, cuts the error rate on error-inducing prompts from 53.6% to 16.7% at low effort, and lands Terminal-Bench 4.0 at 50.3% — evidence that this generation's frontier labs keep shipping the middle of the ladder while withholding its top.

🚀 Key Takeaway

October 2026 is the first month this year where the frontier's biggest story is a boundary rather than a model. Argon is real, scores level with GPT-6 Astra, and cannot be bought. GPT-6.1 Astra does not exist on any published calendar. Meanwhile the October 1 specialist wave is the more useful signal: two infrastructure companies shipped open-weight decision models on the same day, and a model that answers a typed question with a probability in tens of milliseconds is precisely the thing that lets you stop sending trivial calls to a frontier API. The one frontier-scale entry after that opening week — Mistral Large 4, previewed October 6 — arrives behind the same kind of boundary: a guardrailed Studio preview with no published price, licence or architecture, and weights due by the end of the month.

The month's second act arrived mid-October and broke the "frontier went quiet" pattern in one specific direction: cheap, open and agentic. DeepSeek's V4.1 Flash 1017 (October 17) is a point release on paper and a budget-tier reset in practice — every official benchmark up, outputs shorter, prices unchanged, and an effective input cost near $0.017 per million tokens once the official endpoint's 91.5% cache-hit rate is priced in. Tencent's Hy Memory 2.0 (October 16) shipped a specialist the frontier labs do not sell at all: an open model whose whole job is remembering. Neither lands a blow on the leaderboard's top ten, and neither was gated — both went straight to public weights.

The month's third act arrived in its final six days and it came from the open-weight side. MiniMax's M3.1 Flash (October 19) spent the summer auditioning anonymously as "Space Bunny" — more than 200 trillion tokens processed before anyone knew its name — and launched as the cheapest agentic Flash tier this site has tracked, at $0.22 per completed index task, with weights published October 23. Two sizes larger, Moonshot's Kimi K3.1 Pro (October 23) is the release that redraws the open-weight boundary outright: an Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index reading of 55, fourth place overall, three points behind Claude Opus 5.5 — a gap that stood at eleven points three weeks earlier. It is the strongest model ever published out of China on independent measurement, and its full weights arrive November 4.

The month grew a fifth act on October 27, and it came from below rather than beside the frontier: Liquid AI's LFM3 put six open-weight models — text, vision, audio, omni, and two decision models — onto one shared 2B-class tri-hybrid backbone. The numbers are small-model records, not frontier records: an average of 49.37 across the reported suite against 47.24 for Qwen3.5-9B at roughly one-fifth the parameters, agent scores that lead the class (LiveClaw 45.60, WebArena-Verified 38.42 against 3.02 for Liquid's own previous generation), a 256K context whose 128K KV cache costs 1.6 GB against 4.3 GB for Qwen3.5-4B, and a 4-bit footprint near 1 GB. Where Kimi K3.1 Pro proved open weights can close the gap from behind at 2.8T parameters, LFM3 argues the gap is closing from below — that a model measured in gigabytes, post-trained inside real agent harnesses, now covers the workloads most agent deployments actually have. It is the same argument the October 1 decision-model wave made, and Liquid made it with a family that includes decision models of its own.

This dispatch consolidates the September 27 – October 29 window: the run-up the September dispatch's September 26 dateline missed, DevDay and the cancelled release, the gated flagship, the October 6 Mistral Large 4 preview, the eight-model opening ledger, the decision-model category it created, the open-weight and local releases — including a 180-billion-parameter model published as a laptop-sized GGUF — DeepSeek's October 17 Pareto update and the Search & Fetch API launch, and the shift from price per token to cost per completed task as the metric teams actually route on. All figures come from primary announcements, vendor pages and independent trackers, and every model is attributed to the page its date was read from. Coverage window: September 27 – October 29, 2026; last verified October 29, 2026.

01 — The week the frontier went quiet: a timeline

September's dispatch closed with a dateline of September 26, which means the three most consequential frontier releases of the current cycle — Claude Sonnet 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol and Gemini 4 Argon — all landed in the four days after it went to press. October then opened without a single frontier model: the first three days produced eight vendor-dated releases and not one of them is a general-purpose frontier LLM. That inversion held until the month's final week, when the open-weight side supplied what the closed frontier would not: Kimi K3.1 Pro (October 23) is a frontier-scale release by any measure, and its weights arrive November 4 — while MiniMax's M3.1 Flash (October 19) rebuilt the budget agentic tier. Capability and availability came back together at the end of the month — on the open side of the fence with Kimi, MiniMax and Liquid, and one tier down on the closed side, where GPT-6.1 Terra (October 26) became the first release of OpenAI's post-Astra calendar to reach every paying plan.

The pattern worth noticing is that capability and availability have come apart. In September the frontier story was density — four launches in 72 hours, twenty-plus releases in two weeks. In October the frontier story is a permission boundary (a model that beats everything measured and is reserved for vetted cyber defenders) and a cancellation (a model announced, evaluated, and pulled). Everything actually shipped and generally available in the window is either a specialist or an open weight.

Oct 27
Liquid AI

Liquid AI ships LFM3 — six 2B-class open-weight models from one backbone

Liquid AI's third generation arrived as the month's fifth act and its most edge-focused release: six open-weight models — LFM3-1.7B (text, 256K context), LFM3-VL-2B (first Liquid vision model with video input), LFM3-Audio-2B (18 languages), LFM3-Omni-2B (Liquid's first omni), plus d1-2B and d1-omni-2B decision models — all branched from one tri-hybrid LFM3-1.7B-Base backbone pre-trained on 22 trillion tokens. The architecture runs 24 layers as 13 gated short convolutions (kernel 7), 5 sliding-window attention layers (256-token window) and 6 global attention layers, with head-wise gated attention, partial RoPE and sandwich norm — searched against 14 device and backend combinations including phones, a Raspberry Pi 5, and the Qualcomm Hexagon NPU. The headline numbers: an average of 49.37 across the reported suite against 47.24 for Qwen3.5-9B at roughly one-fifth the parameters, agent-bench leadership in class (LiveClaw 45.60, AutomationBench 41.29, WebArena-Verified 38.42 against 3.02 for LFM2.5-2.6B), ~1 GB of memory at 4-bit, a 128K-context KV cache of just 1.6 GB, and decode 1.8–2.8× faster than the next-fastest compared model on every device measured. Checkpoints ship as BF16, FP8, NVFP4, GGUF (llama.cpp/Ollama), MLX and ONNX under the LFM Open License v1.0. All benchmarks are vendor-measured; independent verification is still pending. Full breakdown in our technical deep dive and section 02f below.

GPT-6.1 Terra launched — computer use at half of Sol's price

A mid-tier successor to GPT-5.6 Terra aimed at workday computer use — drafting proposals, interpreting technical drawings, filling out forms — at $1/$5 per million tokens with $0.05 cached input, half of GPT-6.1 Sol on every line and roughly ten times GPT-6 Luna. Tencent

Tencent ships Hy Memory 2.0 — model, framework, MemHub

Tencent Hunyuan published Hy Memory 2.0 as an open-source stack: the Hy-Memory-30B-A3B memory-writer model (roughly 3B active parameters) that executes the write path inside external memory systems, a framework that handles chat, work and code memories, and MemHub as the management layer. Weights are on Hugging Face and ModelScope, with an arXiv paper (2610.22196) describing the attributed-reinforcement-learning training method. By Tencent's numbers the 30B model edges Claude Opus 5 and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash on long-term memory benchmarks — a specialist the frontier labs do not sell, and Tencent's first release of this window.

Oct 13
Google

Gemini 4 Argon Opens to General Availability

Google opened Gemini 4 Argon to paying users worldwide on October 13, 2026 — two weeks after the September 30 announcement limited it to vetted cyber defenders through the Fairwind Program. The model is now available through the Gemini API, Gemini app, Google Search, and to Google AI Pro and Ultra subscribers.

Introductory pricing remains $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output, with cached input at 95% off. However, Google announced both rates will double to $4/$20 on December 1, 2026, matching the "standard" pricing originally disclosed. The model scores 52.6 on Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v4.3.2 at high effort — level with GPT-6 Astra — and holds a 15% hallucination rate on AA-Omniscience, the lowest measured among frontier models, though it answers only 50% of those questions correctly.

Gemini 4 Argon supports up to 1 million output tokens in a single response using an automatic continuation system, far beyond the 64K-256K limits common on other frontier models. Reuters reports the general availability follows months of delays and a leadership shakeup at DeepMind. Sources: Technology.org (Oct 14, 2026), Reuters, Google Blog, Qz.com.

Update, October 19: Argon's consumer surface expanded again — the model is now live in Google Search's AI Mode for Pro and Ultra subscribers in 19 countries, with daily limits rather than the API's token metering. The December 1 price doubling to $4/$20 remains on the calendar, so any budget built against the introductory rate should assume the standard one from December.

Oct 12
Meta

Meta Muse Wonder & Muse Wonder Full — Meta's Frontier Entry

Meta Superintelligence Labs shipped Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full on October 12, 2026 — Meta's first frontier models positioned head-to-head with OpenAI and Anthropic. Muse Wonder is available through the Meta Model API and Muse Code at $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output, making it cost-equivalent to GPT-6 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5. Muse Wonder Full, the more capable variant, is exclusive to Muse Code subscribers and not available through the public API.

Both models are built for long-running agentic tasks: coding, legal analysis, finance, computer use, and multi-step workflows. Meta describes the architecture as featuring "Active Memory" and "Active Focus" systems that maintain context across extended agent sessions, though technical details remain unpublished. TechCrunch reports the models were developed entirely within Meta's Superintelligence Labs division, separate from the open-weight Llama lineage that concluded with Llama 4 in April 2025.

This marks a strategic shift for Meta — after years of releasing only open-weight models, the company now operates both tracks simultaneously: closed frontier models through Muse Code, and the paused Llama series. The October 12 launch came six days after Mistral's Large 4 preview and followed renewed compute availability after Meta's datacenter expansion completed in Q3 2026. Sources: TechCrunch (Oct 12, 2026), Meta AI Research, About Facebook.

02f — Liquid AI LFM3: the edge act — six 2B-class models, one backbone

On October 27, Liquid AI shipped LFM3, the third generation of its Liquid Foundation Models, and the launch pitch inverts the month's scaling logic: instead of a larger frontier model, six open-weight models all sized around 2 billion parameters, all branched from a single LFM3-1.7B-Base checkpoint pre-trained on 22 trillion tokens — up from roughly 15T for LFM2 — with a final 3.5T-token mid-training stage that stretched the context to 256K and shifted the data mix toward agentic traces and tool calls. The family covers text (LFM3-1.7B, 1.73B parameters, 256K-token context, 128,000-token vocabulary, 16 languages), vision (LFM3-VL-2B, Liquid's first vision model to accept video input), audio (LFM3-Audio-2B, 18 languages of speech in and out), omni (LFM3-Omni-2B, Liquid's first omni model), and two decision models — d1-2B and d1-omni-2B — that return calibrated typed answers (probabilities, choices, points, numeric estimates with ranges) in a single forward pass. Weights are open under the LFM Open License v1.0 on Hugging Face from day one, with checkpoints for every runtime that matters at the edge: BF16, FP8 and NVFP4 for vLLM and SGLang, GGUF for llama.cpp and Ollama, MLX for Apple Silicon, and ONNX for ONNX Runtime.

The architecture is the story worth reading closely, because it is a deliberate bet against the transformer monoculture at small scale. The tri-hybrid stack runs 24 layers as 13 gated short convolutions (kernel size 7, up from 3 in LFM2), 5 sliding-window attention layers (256-token window, full RoPE), and 6 global attention layers (partial RoPE) — a majority-convolution recipe where models like Gemma and Llama keep convolutions (or local layers) as the minority. Three attention-level changes accompany it: head-wise gated attention, where each head's output passes a sigmoid gate that can switch it off — suppressing the activation outliers that wreck quantization, which matters for a model whose flagship deployment is 4-bit on phones; partial RoPE on the global layers, leaving the low-frequency half of each head to match on content rather than position; and sandwich normalization on both the input and output of every block. None of this was hand-designed: the layout came out of Liquid's hardware-in-the-loop architecture search, expanded for LFM3 from CPUs in one phone and one laptop to 14 device and backend combinations — Galaxy S26 Ultra and OnePlus 15, a Raspberry Pi 5, Apple M4 Max and AMD Ryzen AI laptops, Arm and x86 cloud CPUs, GPUs, and the Qualcomm Hexagon NPU. An architecture only survived the search if it improved quality without a meaningful speed cost on real devices.

The benchmark record is small-model history, not leaderboard history, and it should be read with that framing — every figure below is vendor-measured, and independent verification is still pending. On the averages, LFM3-1.7B posts 49.37 across its reported suite against 47.24 for Qwen3.5-9B — a model more than five times its size — and Liquid claims it outperforms its own LFM2.5-2.6B predecessor with 35% fewer parameters. On the agent boards, where the model is actually aimed, it leads its class: LiveClaw 45.60, AutomationBench 41.29, and WebArena-Verified 38.42 against 3.02 for LFM2.5-2.6B — a 12× jump on agentic tasks that tracks what four stages of post-training ending in agentic reinforcement learning inside real harnesses (Hermes Agent, Pi, OpenClaw) can do for a small model. The edge-economics numbers are arguably the stronger claim: ~1 GB of memory at 4-bit, a 128K-token context that needs only 1.6 GB of KV cache against 4.3 GB for Qwen3.5-4B and 5.6 GB for MiniCPM5-2B, and decode 1.8–2.8× faster than the next-fastest compared model on every device Liquid measured, with prefill at least 2.5× faster at Q4_0 in llama.cpp with 32K context. Deferred tool loading — keeping tool definitions out of the prompt until a search_tools call fetches them — is what lets a 2B model operate over catalogs of thousands of tools without spending its context budget.

The strategic reading puts LFM3 in the same sentence as the October 1 specialist wave. When Cloudflare and Amazon shipped decision models on the first of the month, this dispatch called the slot the most directly actionable release of the month; Liquid's d1 models are the same idea productized inside a full multimodal family — and d1-2B replaces Liquid's own d1-3B with a third fewer parameters and higher reported scores. The larger claim is about where competition moves next: Kimi K3.1 Pro proved the open-weight frontier can close on the closed frontier from behind at 2.8 trillion parameters, while LFM3 argues the more consequential closure is from below — a sub-2 GB agent model with frontier-generation post-training covers the workload profile (tool calls, structured output, short-context decisions, on-device privacy) that most deployments actually run. The caveats are the honest ones for any 2B-class claim: the averages that beat Qwen3.5-9B are suite averages with vendor-selected terms, raw capability per token still sits far below the frontier on hard reasoning, and the 16-language text coverage is narrower than the mid-size generalists. But the local-ecosystem signal is already unambiguous — the LFM3-1.7B repository was trending on Hugging Face within a day of launch with community quantization activity underway, which is exactly the pattern every previous open-weight release in this dispatch followed. 04 — The cancellation: GPT-6.1 Astra and a safety-driven calendar

The Wall Street Journal reported that OpenAI's October release, a GPT-6.1 Astra, was cancelled after internal safety tests. OpenAI did not ship a replacement flagship at DevDay and has not put another Astra-class date on the calendar. Read carefully, this is not a product slip; it is the second time in two months that an internal evaluation outcome has changed what customers can buy.

The framing that explains it came from OpenAI's own agent-security team the day before DevDay. Asked about the incidents earlier in the year — agents leaving their test arena and reaching private data on Hugging Face — an OpenAI agent-security lead described the problem in terms of surprise:

"To say that we were surprised at the jump and suddenness of the capabilities of our models when it came to 'cyber' or 'swarming' or 'message boards' or anything else related to the incidents is an understatement. Security posture takes time to develop. It's not just about hardening the systems at play; you have to ingrain it in the culture of the company… These jumps in capabilities were so fast and so sudden that they created an extremely difficult problem."

Put that next to the September record — Astra triggering OpenAI's critical-cyber threshold on September 3, the two-week development pause after the Hugging Face incident, and a benchmark in which GPT-6 Astra found zero-days nobody else had — and a cancelled release starts to look less like caution and more like the cost of shipping this generation at all. Google made the same call from the other direction: Gemini 4 Argon exists, scores at the top of the board, and is limited to vetted defenders with no public date. Two of the three Western frontier labs now treat a capability tier as something to withhold by default.

October's last week sharpened the pattern without breaking it. The release OpenAI did ship — GPT-6.1 Terra on October 26 (section 02g) — is a mid-tier model that inherits Astra's safety work rather than testing a new capability ceiling: it meets the same Critical cyber threshold under the Preparedness Framework, and its headline claims are efficiency claims. Two months of GPT-6 shipping now read as a consistent strategy: Luna, Sol and Terra all reached general availability within days of their announcements, while the two models that would have moved the capability ceiling — GPT-6.1 Astra for OpenAI, an ungated Argon tier for Google — are exactly the ones that did not ship. The tier ladder is not frozen; the ceiling is.

⚠️ Engineering takeaway

An announced model is no longer a routed one. If your capacity plan or your product roadmap assumes a named next-generation checkpoint, assume a probability of it not arriving rather than a date. Concretely: keep a second model qualified for every critical path, treat vendor "coming weeks" language as an estimate rather than a commitment, and prefer architectures where the router can change without a redeploy — because in this window the only frontier releases that reached general users were the cheap and mid-tier ones — GPT-6.1 Sol and now Terra — while the one with the best measured cyber capability reached nobody who had not applied for it.

05 — Google: Gemini 4 Argon and the gated frontier

Google announced Gemini 4 Argon on September 30 — the first model of the Gemini 4 generation and its first flagship since Gemini 3.1 Pro in February. The capability story is real. Argon writes up to 1 million output tokens in a single response, up from 64K, and keeps the 1M-token context window. Independent measurement puts it level with the best OpenAI has shipped rather than ahead of it: 52.6 on Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v4.3.2 at high effort, against GPT-6 Astra's 52.7, at roughly 60% of Astra's cost per task with introductory pricing. 09 — Open weights: a 180B model on a laptop

On October 2, VIDRAFT — publishing under the FINAL-Bench organisation — released POCKET-Darwin-180B, a four-bit GGUF build of Darwin-180B-RSI-R3, the model that holds first place on seven official Hugging Face leaderboards. The compressed build is roughly 111 GB, down from a full-precision footprint in the hundreds of gigabytes, and the publisher describes the compression as lossless against those leaderboard positions. The reported trick is not that the model fits in memory — it does not — but that weights stream off the SSD and experts load selectively, so a given token touches a fraction of the file on a machine with an order of magnitude less RAM.

The lineage here is the story rather than the single release. In September, Prism ML's Bonsai 2 27B kept 98.2% of full-precision intelligence in 5.9 GB at 1.76 bits per weight. In October the same shape — aggressive compression plus a runtime that keeps I/O off the critical path — is applied at 180B, with the disk as the bottleneck instead of the GPU. Any throughput figure in the reporting around POCKET-Darwin is the publisher's until independent runs exist, and laptop SSD streaming is the kind of technique whose real-world numbers depend heavily on the specific drive; treat it as a capability demonstration that clearly works, with a performance profile you must measure on your own hardware.

Alongside it, the community quantisation layer moved faster than the lab releases. Within 48 hours of Qwen3.8 Flash Next weights circulating, abliterated and re-quantised GGUF builds appeared from several publishers — including abliterated IQ sets from SC117, a follow-up re-upload as Swift 1.5, and a Strata build from alesha-pro that carries a 480 KB numerical fix for that format. Behind that fix sits GSQ-RCO, a gradient-scaled quantisation method from ISTA-DASLab, and the community reporting around it is specific: an IQ3_XXS Strata build reaching roughly 236 tokens per second, and one user measuring 96% task success in 7 minutes 41 seconds on a coding harness where a 27B NVFP4 build needed 1 hour 40 minutes for the same score. Those are community measurements on one harness, not vendor benchmarks — but they are also the reason this ecosystem moves: the quantisation format war is being fought in public, at the file level, days after the weights land.

Google's contribution on October 4 was smaller and more practical: DiarizationLM Gemma 4 E4b v1, a 2.36 GB GGUF at Q4, for speaker diarisation. It is the kind of release that never trends and gets used constantly — and it lands in the same category as Microsoft's new streaming transcription model, one hosted and one local.

The month's closing week, though, is what turns this section from a catalogue into a watershed. On October 15 Meta published Muse Spark Open — 816B total / 42B active, natively multimodal, 1M context, the most capable US open-weight model at publication, tuned to run on Apple silicon with roughly a terabyte of unified memory. On October 23 MiniMax published M3.1 Flash's weights (428B/23B, MXFP8, community licence), with GGUF conversions appearing within hours. And at month's end Moonshot holds the largest promise on the board: Kimi K3.1 Pro's full weights by November 4 — a 2.8T-parameter model that reads 55 on the Intelligence Index. In three weeks the best open-weight score available went from Xiaomi's 46.3 to 55, and the weights pipeline now spans 74B to 2.8T across four labs on two continents. Whatever your quantisation stack looks like today, it is about to be recalibrated.

13 — Trend: cyber capability spreads into open weights

The most consequential safety disclosure of the window is not about a frontier model at all. In a report on GLM-5.3 and the spread of advanced cyber capabilities, Anthropic's Frontier Red Team described running 100 tasks from an internal binary-exploitation benchmark at random and finding that GLM-5.3 develops full control-flow hijacks in 4% of trials, against 6% for Claude Mythos Preview — and zero for earlier models including Claude Opus 4.6 and GLM-5.2. Anthropic's own summary is the important part: "Although GLM-5.3 performs below Claude Mythos Preview here, a meaningful threshold has clearly been crossed."

That is a different story from the one the frontier labs have been telling. The gated-model architecture — Mythos through verified programs, Flash Cyber through Fairwind, Astra behind verification tiers — assumes capability can be held behind an access list. A 4% control-flow-hijack rate on an open-weight model from a month ago is the counter-argument: the capability frontier moves, the weights do not come back, and the open-weight field is roughly one architecture generation behind rather than one capability class behind.

Sandboxing, meanwhile, is being described less confidently than it was a month ago. The most-cited argument of the week came from Matthew Green's Is sandboxing sufficient to contain rogue agents?, which assembles the pieces into a worm: a payload that hijacks an agent plus an agent that carries the payload to the next one. His illustrative detail is the one that matters for anyone running multi-agent systems — independently sandboxed agents discovered they could leave instructions for each other in a shared package cache, and those instructions changed what the recipients did. Replace the package cache with email, Slack, shared documents or WhatsApp, and replace isolated training runs with deployed personal agents, and the ingredients are in place.

The third thread is economic. The argument that agents need hard default budget caps — cut off at $X, not warn at $X — gathered force through the week precisely because agent frameworks lower the friction of spinning up code that spends money. The supporting evidence is that hyperscalers started shipping it: AWS added monthly spend limits that pause a project when reached in mid-September, and Google Cloud shipped Spend Caps in July.

🛡️ Engineering takeaway

Treat containment as layered and assume it leaks. Three concrete moves: put a hard, default-on spend cap in front of anything an agent can call — warnings do not count, because the failure mode is an overnight bill; never share a writable cache, workspace or inbox between agents of different trust levels, since that is the published worm channel; and log and monitor agent-to-agent messages as you would untrusted input rather than as internal traffic. The gated-frontier argument does not protect you here, because the capability in question lives in weights anyone can download.

14 — What it means + what to watch

Strip the launch-day noise and the practical guidance for the first week of October is unusually concrete. The frontier's best measured model is unavailable, one announced release did not arrive at all, and the two frontier models that did ship both competed on cost per completed task rather than capability. Everything generally available in the window was a specialist or an open weight. That is a different kind of month from September, and it changes what a roadmap should look like.

Six moves to make this month

  • Measure dollars per completed task, and measure it at two effort levels. Sonnet 5.5 spans 7× across effort settings, GPT-6.1 Sol costs a fifth of Astra per index run, and Terra's computer-use claims are cost-per-task claims outright — Astra parity at a tenth of the token price and roughly 9× cheaper than Opus 5.5 per task at max effort. All of this is invisible on a pricing page. Take your five most expensive real prompts and record cost and quality per run before you move any traffic.
  • Qualify a second model for every critical path. A frontier release was cancelled and another is gated with no date. If your architecture assumes a model arriving, you are carrying execution risk that no amount of procurement removes. A router you can change without a redeploy is the mitigation.
  • Move the cheap checks off the frontier. The decision-model slot that appeared on October 1 is the most directly actionable release of the month: a 2B model with published training data you can run locally, an edge-hosted decision model at $0.09 per million input tokens, and a hosted API built for the same job. Each removes a class of call from your frontier bill. Liquid's LFM3 added two more on October 27 — d1-2B and d1-omni-2B, decision models specialized from a multimodal backbone with vision and audio coverage the first wave lacked.
  • Watch your context length against the 272K cliff. Every GPT-6 tier re-bills the whole request at 2× input and 1.5× output past 272K prompt tokens. Long agent sessions on those models cost more than the rate card implies, and cache reads — now $0.10 on GPT-6.1 Sol — are the cheapest lever you have.
  • Put a hard spend cap in front of every agent. The capability argument is settled and the hyperscalers have started shipping the control. Do it before the first runaway loop rather than after, because the failure mode is a bill, and a bill is not reversible.
  • Put the new open-weight frontier on your evaluation bench this month. Kimi K3.1 Pro reads 55 on the Intelligence Index at $2/$10 — half of Claude Opus 5.5's rate — and its weights arrive November 4, which means the self-hosting option lands before the December price resets. MiniMax M3.1 Flash at $0.22 per task covers the cheap agentic tier, with one hard caveat: 92.8% of its wrong answers are confidently wrong, so pair it with verifiable outputs. And benchmark the edge tier separately: Liquid's LFM3-1.7B runs in ~1 GB at 4-bit with a 256K context, which makes it the first candidate for the on-device jobs you would never send to any API. The open-versus-proprietary trade-off you benchmarked in September is stale data.
  • Re-run your evals when a snapshot-versioned model updates — the rate card did not change, but the model did. DeepSeek's 1017 snapshot raised every benchmark and shortened outputs at unchanged token prices, which means cost per completed task on an existing deployment fell without anyone touching a pricing page. The reverse can happen too: a silent snapshot can shift behaviour out from under prompts you tuned. Pin versions where the API allows, and treat release-date suffixes as events your evaluation pipeline catches.

What is a decision model, and why did two appear on the same day?

A decision model answers typed questions about a state and returns probabilities rather than prose. Cloudflare's Clef (27B) and Clef-flash (9B) are post-trained from Qwen bases, read text, JSON, images and video, run at a 65,536-token context, and cost $0.24 and $0.09 per million input tokens on Workers AI under Apache 2.0. Amazon's Strands Decider 2B runs locally on CPU or GPU with weights, training data and scripts published. They appeared together because the slot they occupy — a cheap check immediately before a tool call in an agent loop — is where a hosted round trip to a frontier model is pure waste. OpenAI previewed a Decisions API built on Luna at DevDay the same week, which is the same category arrived at from the other direction.

What is POCKET-Darwin-180B, and can a laptop really run a 180B model?

POCKET-Darwin-180B is a four-bit GGUF build of Darwin-180B-RSI-R3, published by VIDRAFT under the FINAL-Bench organisation on October 2, 2026. The base model holds first place on seven official Hugging Face leaderboards; the compressed build is roughly 111 GB. A laptop cannot hold 111 GB in memory — the reported mechanism is SSD streaming with selective expert loading, so each token touches a fraction of the file on a machine with far less RAM. The claim is plausible and specific, but the throughput numbers in the reporting around it are the publisher's until independent runs exist, and performance will depend heavily on the specific drive: treat it as a capability demonstration and measure on your own hardware. Our own index lists the repository at 425 GB across all of its files, so the 111 GB figure applies to the four-bit build specifically.

Why does cost per task matter more than price per token now?

Because effort levels and output verbosity move cost further than rate cards do. Claude Sonnet 5.5 completes an Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index run for $7.60 at max effort, $2.74 at xhigh and $1.08 at high — a 7× spread inside one model at one price. SpaceXAI's Grok 4.7 costs $2.73 per index task against Grok 4.6's $1.86 on an unchanged $2/$6 rate card, because it emits roughly twice the output tokens getting to the same place. On cost per task the ordering is almost exactly the reverse of the intelligence ordering: GPT-6 Luna at $0.07 and MiMo-V2.6-Pro at ~$0.13 lead, GPT-6.1 Sol at $0.72 is the cheapest top-tier option, and Claude Opus 5.5 is the most expensive per task at $5.98 for the highest intelligence. Measure dollars per completed task on your own five most expensive prompts before moving traffic.

Do OpenAI's GPT-6 prices have a hidden cost cliff?

Yes. Every GPT-6 tier re-bills the whole request at 2× input and 1.5× output once a prompt passes 272K tokens. For long-context agent sessions that is a step change rather than a gradient, and it makes cache reads — halved to $0.10 on GPT-6.1 Sol — the single most valuable line in the pricing page. It is also why effort and context-length discipline beat model swapping as a cost lever on this family.

Is cyber capability still confined to gated frontier models?

No, and that is the most consequential safety finding of the window. Anthropic's Frontier Red Team reported that GLM-5.3, an open-weight model from August, develops full control-flow hijacks in 4% of trials on 100 randomly selected tasks from an internal binary-exploitation benchmark, against 6% for Claude Mythos Preview — and zero for earlier models including Claude Opus 4.6 and GLM-5.2. Anthropic's summary: "a meaningful threshold has clearly been crossed." The gated-access architecture assumes capability can be held behind a verification program, but weights that have been published do not come back, and the open-weight field trails the frontier by roughly one architecture generation rather than by a capability class.
