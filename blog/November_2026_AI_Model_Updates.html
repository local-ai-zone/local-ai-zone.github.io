November 2026 AI Model Updates: Every Launch, Access Change, and Price Move

TL;DR — November 2026 did not stay quiet. The first week was a trickle: a restricted research model, a billing clock and the Kimi weight drop. The next ten days produced the busiest frontier stretch of the fall. On November 9, Anthropic shipped Claude Fable 5.2 and its restricted twin Mythos 5.2 at $8/$40, and Fable took No. 1 on Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index v5 at 65 — still first on November 19, but no longer by a comfortable margin. November 10 ended the Gemini 4 Carbon gate: Google made Carbon generally available at a $2/$10 introductory rate (doubling to $4/$20 on December 1), and it took first on CursorBench 4.0. November 12 added Gemini 4 Hydrogen at $1.50/$7.50 with a free tier. November 16 brought Qwen 4 Max at $1.50/$4.50 — 43 on the index, API-only, with no dated open-weight commitment. November 17 brought OpenAI back to the frontier: GPT-6.2 Astra at $8/$40, 62 on the index, and GPT-6.2 Sol at $2/$10, 57. November 18 closed with Gemini 4 Neon at $3/$15, 61. Six 50-plus releases in ten days, and a leaderboard whose top four sit within five points of each other.

The number that matters more than 65 is $4.00. That is Artificial Analysis' measured average cost per index task for GPT-6.2 Astra at max effort — three index points below Fable 5.2's 65 and roughly one-seventh of Fable's $26.54. GPT-6.2 Sol scores 57 at $1.01 per task. Gemini 4 Neon scores 61 at $6.44; Carbon 60 at $7.76; Hydrogen 54 at $3.16. The intelligence frontier and the intelligence-per-dollar frontier are now in different places, and for most workloads the second one is the routing decision.

The month's other correction is about open weights. Moonshot published Kimi K3.1 Pro's full 2.8T weights on November 4, on schedule — but under the Kimi K3.1 Pro License, a custom licence with a model-as-a-service revenue clause, not Apache 2.0. Hugging Face reports 60,920 downloads last month, 23 quantizations and derivatives, and llama.cpp conversions now exist; the earlier claim that no GGUF had appeared is obsolete. On the current v5 table Kimi reads 46 — still the highest open-weight score, but 19 points behind the frontier, not the 55 that earlier coverage reported. Mistral Large 4's weights also shipped, on October 29, two days inside its commitment: 1.05T total, 52B active, under Mistral's ecosystem open-weight licence.

And the month produced a capital-markets event to match the models: on November 18, nine days after Fable 5.2 launched, Anthropic priced its IPO at $83 a share, upsized to 980 million shares for roughly $81 billion raised and a valuation near $1.5 trillion. It began trading on Nasdaq as ANT on November 19, the dateline of this dispatch.

This edition replaces the November 11 version and corrects it: Carbon is not gated, Qwen 4 did not ship at 58 for $1/$4 on November 11, Kimi's licence is not Apache 2.0, Mistral did not miss its weight date, and the GGUF ledger's top line was wrong. The corrections are listed explicitly below. Coverage window: October 30 – November 19, 2026; every figure carries the date it was read.

🚀 Key Takeaway

November's frontier did not consolidate around one winner; it split into a capability race and a cost race, and the cost race is moving faster. Claude Fable 5.2 still holds No. 1 at 65, and its capability story — the two deliberate training caps, the 1,000-agent operating-system demo, the 30-hour Fermat formalization — remains the most consequential governance story of the year. But the table around it changed shape in ten days. GPT-6.2 Astra arrived three points back at roughly one-seventh the measured cost per task; Gemini 4 Neon arrived two points further back at about a quarter of Fable's measured cost; Carbon and Hydrogen gave Google a complete ladder from $1.50 to $3 input; and GPT-6.2 Sol put a 57 on the board for about a dollar a task. A three-point lead that costs 6.6× per completed task is a different product from a three-point lead that costs roughly the same, and buyers will treat it that way.

The second story is delivery. Kimi K3.1 Pro's weights landed on the promised date, Mistral Large 4's weights landed two days inside its promised window, and Google converted Carbon from a benchmark reading into a purchasable model with a published price and a December cliff. The promises that did not resolve were the ones without dates: Qwen 4 Max shipped without a weight schedule for the Max tier, and xAI's Grok 4.8 slipped again. The lesson from October — a dated commitment is a different asset from a roadmap — held in both directions.

The third story is local. The November GGUF ledger is deeper than the November 11 edition reported: 148 uploads across 23 distinct models, led by mradermacher's DeepSeek V4 Pro 9B heretic build at 624,767 downloads, with ISTA-DASLab's DeepSeek V4.1 Flash coder build at 441,751 and Hyperflow H3 Flash at 496,060. None is a frontier base model; all are the community layer doing what it does — compressing, uncensoring, and re-packaging the releases the labs will not ship small. Kimi's 2.8T weights are now quantizable, but they remain data-centre work, not laptop work.

02 — Anthropic: Claude Fable 5.2 and the two-regime frontier

On Monday, November 9, 2026, Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 alongside its restricted twin Claude Mythos 5.2 — the fifth generation of a structure invented on June 9, when Fable 5 became the first publicly available model in the Mythos class: one set of weights, two products, with Fable carrying production safeguards and general availability while Mythos reserves the unrestricted system for vetted organizations. The 5.2 generation keeps the structure and sharpens the boundary. Mythos 5.2 is the full-strength system, and Anthropic describes it as its most capable model for cybersecurity engineering. Fable 5.2 is what everyone else gets: the same core model with additional guardrails over sensitive domains, most visibly cybersecurity, where the guardrail layer blocks most security-offensive task categories outright.

At the November 19 reading, Fable 5.2 is No. 1 of 125 models on Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index v5 at 65. That is still first, but the cushion described in the November 11 edition did not survive the next ten days. GPT-6.2 Astra is three points back at 62, Gemini 4 Neon four back at 61, and Gemini 4 Carbon five back at 60. The gap is now small enough that the more important number is the one in the cost column: Artificial Analysis measures Fable 5.2 at $26.54 per index task at max effort, against $4.00 for Astra, $6.44 for Neon and $7.76 for Carbon. Fable still wins the capability table. It no longer wins it by enough to ignore a 6.6× cost difference.

The gains over Opus 5.5 are real but unevenly distributed, and the distribution is the story. Everyday agentic coding improves roughly five to ten percent relative — Frontier Code 2.0 moves from 45.5% to 49.1%, Frontier SWE v2 from 62.3% to 69.9% — while the hard, specialized domains jump further: Terminal-Bench Science 0.1 goes from 58.7% to 74.6%, and Anthropic's BenchCAD 2.1 reading moves from 65.3% for Opus 5.5 to 83.5% for Fable 5.2. Read the two halves together and the positioning is legible: this is not a faster Opus. It is a specialist for long-horizon engineering and formal work, priced like one. Anthropic's own guidance points most coding and knowledge work at Opus 5.5 and treats Fable 5.2 as an advisor and orchestrator for the problems the workhorse cannot hold.

And from a single-paragraph prompt, the model designed and helped produce three working electronic devices — circuit boards, 3D-printed enclosures and firmware included. On the safety side, the 5.2 system card covers both models in one document, and the alignment results are, unusually for a capability jump, mostly good news: the models matched or outperformed Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Mythos 5.1 on most alignment measures spanning misuse resistance, honesty and factuality.

Where it loses, honestly: cost and general-purpose fit. At $26.54 per measured task, Fable 5.2 is the most expensive model in the top group, and the models three to five points behind it now cost between one-seventh and one-third as much per completed task. Carbon, generally available since November 10, is the better fit for several business-workflow and coding workloads; Opus 5.5 remains the recommended daily driver at half the per-token price; and GPT-6.2 Astra has turned the frontier into a two-horse race again. The correct mental model is a specialist: reach for 5.2 on long-horizon agentic engineering, formal science, hardware design and orchestration over fleets of cheaper models — and route the routine work elsewhere.

02b — The 5.2 contract: what breaks for developers

The API contract is where 5.2's engineering reality lives, and the changes relative to Fable 5.1 are modest but real — one of them touches every account. The Fable-line rules carry over intact: adaptive thinking is the only thinking mode and cannot be disabled (sending thinking: {"type": "disabled"} or a budget_tokens value returns a 400), forced tool use is gone (tool_choice types any and tool return a 400 — use auto with strict schemas or explicit instructions), and sampling parameters must simply be omitted: temperature, top_p and top_k are 400 errors on this model. Depth of reasoning is controlled solely through the effort parameter, which defaults to high on the API and to medium in the Claude apps. That split matters: the previous edition said the default moved to high everywhere, which overstates the cost change for subscription surfaces.

The binding change that matters for every account: Fable 5.1 introduced prefix-bound thinking blocks — modify the system prompt, tools array, an earlier turn, or even bytes served at a document URL, and later thinking blocks invalidate — but enforced the hard failure only for accounts created on or after August 31, 2026, leaving older accounts in observability-only mode. On Fable 5.2 the enforcement is universal. If your stack still edits earlier turns — injecting reminders, reordering history, mutating tool results — patterns that ran silently on 5.1 will start returning 400s the day you swap the model ID. Refusals are also not HTTP errors: Fable 5.2 returns HTTP 200 with stop_reason: "refusal" and a stop_details object naming the policy area, unbilled when nothing was emitted. Error handlers written for Opus's behavior should treat refusal as a routing event, not an exception.

Speed and pricing move in the customer's favor. Output generation is up to 30% faster than Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1, and a new fast mode — available on Claude Code, the Claude Platform, Google Cloud and Microsoft Foundry — trades roughly double the per-token price ($16/$80) for up to 2.5× faster output. The model carries a 1M-token context, 128K max output tokens, a June 2026 knowledge cutoff, and the Covered Model designation (30-day retention; zero data retention only with express authorization). One-directional thinking compatibility carries over: Fable 5.2 can read thinking blocks from earlier Claude models, so migrating a conversation onto 5.2 preserves its reasoning history; falling back from 5.2 to Opus 5.5 or older drops the reasoning for turns that ran on 5.2.

02c — The capability caps, and what Anthropic refused to build

The most consequential part of this release is not a benchmark — it is a sentence. Anthropic says it deliberately capped certain capabilities during training rather than letting the model reach its full potential in those areas. The two named domains are accelerator kernel optimization and improving large language model architectures. Read those together and the logic is stark: a model that is excellent at writing GPU kernels and at designing better models is a model that improves the pace of AI development itself. Anthropic throttled exactly the capability that would compress its own R&D loop, and did so in training — a choice that costs capability permanently rather than gating it at runtime.

The scoping deserves precision, because early coverage garbled it. The caps apply to two specific domains; agentic coding, hardware development and traditional AI research — including interpretability — remain intact. The Fermat formalization, the hardware design work and the OS build all proceed at full capability. Context that makes the choice credible rather than theatrical: Anthropic says Claude writes over 90% of the changes merged into its own core codebase under human guidance. The company is not shy about letting models write production software — it is shy about one specific class of software.

The competitive question has changed since November 11. Carbon is no longer a gated reading; Google made it generally available on November 10 at $2/$10 introductory pricing, and it sits fourth on the v5 table at 60. What Google has not published is a comparable statement about training-time caps. That silence is now the most interesting open question in AI governance: a cap is a competitive sacrifice only if rivals do not quietly decline to make it. Fable 5.2's caps may be a genuine attempt to slow one specific feedback loop, or they may be a unilateral handicap in a race where the other leaders have not agreed to the same rules. The system card documents Anthropic's choice; it cannot document anyone else's.

⚠️ Engineering takeaway

The caps do not change what you can build with Fable 5.2 — they change what you can assume about the roadmap. If your planning model assumes each generation's model can accelerate the next generation's training, Anthropic has published that its own loop is deliberately not running at full speed in two named domains. Budget accordingly: the capability curve you are riding is the capped one, and the uncapped capability — wherever it exists — is behind someone else's verification wall.

04 — Kimi K3.1 Pro: the weights, the licence, and the quants that arrived

October's dispatch called Kimi K3.1 Pro's weight drop the event that would turn the month's highest open-weight score into a quantisation wave, and put it on or by November 4. Moonshot hit the date. The licence is the correction that matters most. That is a real permission boundary, not a formality: a startup can download the weights, but a hosted inference business cannot simply resell them without talking to Moonshot. The November 11 edition's “Apache 2.0” claim was wrong, and the error changed the commercial calculus for exactly the readers most likely to act on it.

06 — The edge organizes: LFM3-Harness

October 27 gave the edge the models (Liquid AI's LFM3 family — six 2B-class open-weight models on one tri-hybrid backbone, ~1 GB at 4-bit); November 1–2 gave the edge the pattern. LFM3-Harness is an independent, MIT-licensed community project — explicitly not affiliated with Liquid AI — published as v0.1.1 with a project site and GitHub repository, and it is the first complete wiring of Liquid's family into a supervised local agent stack: a d1-2B decision model as router, an LFM3-1.7B actor, LFM3-VL-2B for vision and LFM3-Audio-2B for voice, all pinned to CPU llama.cpp servers bound to 127.0.0.1 — no cloud calls anywhere in the loop.

The supervision machinery is the part worth stealing even if you never run the stack. A 75%-floor preflight write gate suppresses approval cards while it holds; frozen SHA-256 acceptance contracts define what “done” means before the work starts; finish claims are refused until the file actually exists on disk; hints are separated from authority so a prompt-injection hint cannot grant permissions; and allow-once approvals expire. The project's own README is honest about the unfinished parts — and its own benchmark run caught the router silently faking itself, the exact failure mode the harness exists to catch. That last fact is the best single argument for the whole approach: the defect was found by the acceptance machinery, not by a user.

This site covered the project in three pieces the week it shipped — a source-level deep dive (November 1), a setup guide (November 2) covering the hash check, the 4.9 GB weight fetch and the one mission that has passed end-to-end acceptance, and seven verification patterns portable to your own agent. The strategic reading: October's dispatch argued the frontier's most consequential competition had moved to the edge, where the units are gigabytes and milliseconds. November's evidence is that the edge community agrees — and is building the trust machinery the big labs sell as products, on a GPU-less laptop, in two weeks, from models that cost about a gigabyte each.

📌 Reading this section honestly

LFM3-Harness is an introduction to machinery, not an endorsement of capability. The project's own materials are explicit that parts are unfinished and the benchmark record is one mission deep. What it demonstrates is feasibility and shape — four sub-2B models, CPU-only, supervised — not that a local stack matches a frontier agent. The honest claim is the one the README makes: the trust patterns work, and they are portable to models you already run.

Why does the LFM3-Harness matter for local AI?

An independent, MIT-licensed community project published November 1–2, 2026 — not affiliated with Liquid AI — that wires four of Liquid's LFM3 models into a supervised local agent stack: a d1-2B router, an LFM3-1.7B actor, LFM3-VL-2B for vision and LFM3-Audio-2B for voice, all pinned to CPU llama.cpp servers on 127.0.0.1. Its supervision machinery includes a 75%-floor preflight write gate, frozen SHA-256 acceptance contracts, finish claims refused until the file exists on disk, hints separated from authority, and expiring allow-once approvals. Its own benchmark run caught its router silently faking itself — the failure mode the harness exists to catch.
