🚀 Key Takeaway

November 2026 is the month the frontier's top slot changed hands on a measurement the whole industry had to re-read, and the month the open-weight frontier's biggest promise was kept on schedule. Fable 5.2's 65 is the highest reading Artificial Analysis has published on any version of its index, and the debut arrives with something no predecessor carried: a written statement from the vendor about what the model was deliberately not allowed to become good at. The two capped domains — accelerator kernel optimization and LLM-architecture improvement — are the two capabilities that would most directly compress AI R&D itself, and the caps are training-time choices, not runtime guardrails. Whatever one thinks of the choice, it is the first time a frontier release has shipped with its own ceiling published alongside its benchmarks.

The month's second act was a delivery, not a launch. Kimi K3.1 Pro's full weights landed November 4, exactly on the schedule Moonshot published October 23, under an Apache 2.0 licence — turning the open-weight record (55 on the index, fourth place overall at release) into the largest downloadable model ever published: 2.8 trillion total parameters, 104 billion active, Kimi Delta Attention's O(1)-memory design, MXFP4-quantized experts. What has not happened yet is the quantisation wave: as of our November 10 index snapshot, no community GGUF conversion of K3.1 Pro had appeared. The largest quantisation job the community has ever taken on is in progress, and the honest reading of our own ledger is that the local ecosystem is still loading.

The month's third act was the edge. LFM3-Harness — an independent, MIT-licensed community project published November 1–2, two weeks after Liquid AI's LFM3 family — wires four of Liquid's 2B-class models into a supervised local agent stack: a d1-2B router, an LFM3-1.7B actor, a VL-2B vision model and an Audio-2B voice model, all pinned to CPU llama.cpp servers on loopback, with a 75%-floor write gate, frozen SHA-256 acceptance contracts, and finish claims refused until the file exists on disk. Its own benchmark run caught its router silently faking itself — which is exactly the kind of defect the harness exists to catch. October's dispatch argued the frontier's most consequential competition had moved to the edge; November is when the edge started organizing.

The month's last act went to the wire. Qwen 4 went live on November 11 — the day this dispatch was being verified — and it answers the Qwen window with the sharpest price-performance reading the v5 table has produced: 58, third place, at $1/$4 per million tokens, roughly one-ninth of Fable 5.2's measured cost per index task. The catch is October's story repeated: what shipped is the API tier — a 1.2T/56B sparse MoE with omni-native input and a switchable-thinking contract — and the open-weights promise, Apache 2.0 by December 20, is a schedule, not yet a download. The fourth act is a calendar, not a model. Cursor loses access to OpenAI models on November 12. GPT-5.6 Sol's promotional pricing ends November 21. Gemini 4 Argon's introductory $2/$10 — the rate most Argon budgets were built against — expires November 30 and doubles to $4/$20 on December 1. DeepSeek's Search and Fetch APIs began billing October 31 at $0.75 per 1,000 calls. Gemini 3.8 Flash doubles January 1, 2027. Meanwhile the two models the fall's roadmap actually depended on both stayed out of reach: GPT-6.1 Astra remains cancelled, and Gemini 4 Carbon — 60 on the new index, second place — still has no full public release as of November 11. 02 — Anthropic: Claude Fable 5.2 and the two-regime frontier

On Monday, November 9, 2026, Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 alongside its restricted twin Claude Mythos 5.2 — the fifth generation of a structure invented on June 9, 2026, when Fable 5 became the first publicly available model in the Mythos class: one set of weights, two products, with Fable carrying production safeguards and general availability while Mythos reserves the unrestricted system for vetted organizations. The 5.2 generation keeps the structure and sharpens the boundary. Mythos 5.2 is the full-strength system, and Anthropic describes it as its “most capable model for cybersecurity engineering” — it flags more vulnerabilities than any prior Claude while simultaneously getting better at reproducing and fixing them, which is precisely why it is not generally available. Fable 5.2 is what everyone else gets: the same core model with additional guardrails layered over the sensitive domains, most visibly cybersecurity, where the guardrail layer blocks most security-offensive task categories outright.

The release is Anthropic's first frontier launch since CEO Dario Amodei's public call for a coordinated slowdown on capability racing — a call that made shipping anything at all a communications exercise, and which the company answered by shipping with the most explicit self-imposed limits in its history (section 02c). Reuters and CNBC both frame the launch against Anthropic's expected IPO, with CNBC putting the timing bluntly: the models “reclaim the AI performance lead” as the company prepares for a potentially historic offering. The claim is measurable this time. Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index v5 places Fable 5.2 first among all measured models at 65 — five clear of Google's unreleased-full Gemini 4 Carbon at 60, ten clear of Kimi K3.1 Pro's open-weight record at 55, and fifteen clear of Anthropic's own Fable 5.1 at 50 — and reports the model leads “at every compute tier,” meaning the ranking holds even when both competitors and spend are normalized.

The gains over Opus 5.5 are real but unevenly distributed, and the distribution is the story. Everyday agentic coding improves roughly five to ten percent relative — Frontier Code 2.0 moves from 45.5% to 49.1%, Frontier SWE v2 from 62.3% to 69.9% — while the hard, specialized domains jump much further: agentic scientific research leaps from 58.7% (Opus 5.5) to 74.6%, expert academic questions without tools read 80% on HLE-Diamond, and the cyber rows move 14.6 points on Irregular's multi-stage attack suite. Read the two halves together and the positioning is legible: this is not a faster Opus. It is a specialist for the hardest long-horizon work, priced like one — and Anthropic's own guidance agrees, recommending Opus 5.5 for most coding and knowledge work and treating Fable 5.2 as an advisor and orchestrator for the problems the workhorse cannot hold.

More reasoning did not always produce more score — the model hits diminishing returns, and occasionally negative returns, when pushed to think longer.

The demos are the part of the release that benchmarks cannot rank, and Anthropic leaned on three. An Anthropic engineer gave Fable 5.2 an empty folder and a goal — design a prototype operating system — and over eight days the model coordinated more than 1,000 sub-agents to produce one. The model formalized the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in 30 hours, again using roughly 1,000 agents, turning a 129-page human proof into machine-checked form; Opus 5.5 completed only about 26% of the same formalization in the identical timeframe. And from a single-paragraph prompt, the model designed and helped produce three working electronic devices — circuit boards, 3D-printed enclosures, and firmware included. Independent creators testing the release reported the same shape of result at smaller scale. On the safety side, the 5.2 system card covers both models in one document, and the alignment results are, unusually for a capability jump, mostly good news: the models matched or outperformed Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Mythos 5.1 on most alignment measures spanning misuse resistance, honesty and factuality.

Where it loses, honestly: on AA-Briefcase, the knowledge-work benchmark, Fable 5.2 scored 1,785 Elo — behind Claude Sonnet 5.5's 1,813 and within the confidence interval of Opus 5.5's 1,800. Business-workflow benchmarks showed Gemini 4 Carbon edging out Fable by a couple of points. MindStudio's round-up of independent tester reports found Opus 5.5 still produced better visual game design even when Fable nailed the underlying mechanics faster, and Carbon leads the boards Fable's knowledge-work profile should own. The correct mental model is a specialist: reach for 5.2 on long-horizon agentic engineering, formal science, hardware design and orchestration over fleets of cheaper models — and keep the daily driver on Opus 5.5 at half the price.

02b — The 5.2 contract: what breaks for developers

The API contract is where 5.2's engineering reality lives, and the changes relative to Fable 5.1 are modest but real — one of them touches every account. The Fable-line rules carry over intact: adaptive thinking is the only thinking mode and cannot be disabled (sending thinking: {"type": "disabled"} or a budget_tokens value returns a 400), forced tool use is gone (tool_choice types any and tool return a 400 — use auto with strict schemas or explicit instructions), and sampling parameters must simply be omitted: temperature, top_p and top_k are 400 errors on this model. Depth of reasoning is controlled solely through the effort parameter, which now defaults to high across the board per the docs — up from the mixed defaults (high in Claude Code, medium on claude.ai and Cowork) that 5.1 shipped with.

The binding change that matters for every account: Fable 5.1 introduced prefix-bound thinking blocks — modify the system prompt, tools array, an earlier turn, or even bytes served at a document URL, and later thinking blocks invalidate — but enforced the hard failure only for accounts created on or after August 31, 2026, leaving older accounts in observability-only mode. On Fable 5.2 the enforcement is universal. If your stack still edits earlier turns — injecting reminders, reordering history, mutating tool results — patterns that ran silently on 5.1 will start returning 400s the day you swap the model ID. Refusals are also not HTTP errors: Fable 5.2 returns HTTP 200 with stop_reason: "refusal" and a stop_details object naming the policy area, unbilled when nothing was emitted — error handlers written for Opus's behavior should treat refusal as a routing event, not an exception.

Speed and pricing move in the customer's favor. Output generation is up to 30% faster than Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1, and a new fast mode (available on Claude Code, the Claude Platform, Google Cloud and Microsoft Foundry) trades roughly double the per-token price for up to 2.5× faster output — the first time the Fable line has offered a latency-for-cost dial rather than simply being slow. The model carries a 1M-token context, 128K max output tokens, a June 2026 knowledge cutoff, and the Covered Model designation (30-day retention; zero data retention only with express authorization). 02c — The capability caps, and what Anthropic refused to build

The most consequential part of this release is not a benchmark — it is a sentence. Anthropic says it deliberately capped certain capabilities during training rather than letting the model reach its full potential in those areas. The two named domains are accelerator kernel optimization and improving large language model architectures. Read those together and the logic is stark: a model that is excellent at writing GPU kernels and at designing better models is a model that improves the pace of AI development itself. Anthropic throttled exactly the capability that would compress its own R&D loop, and did so in training — a choice that costs capability permanently rather than gating it at runtime.

The scoping deserves precision, because early coverage garbled it. The caps apply to two specific domains; agentic coding, hardware development, and traditional AI research — including interpretability — remain fully intact. The Fermat formalization, the protein-design work and the OS build all proceed at full capability. Context that makes the choice credible rather than theatrical: Anthropic simultaneously claims that Claude writes over 90% of the changes merged into its own core codebase under human guidance. The company is not shy about letting models write production software — it is shy about one specific class of software. Independent evaluation organizations, as reported around this release, have concluded the caps are real; what nobody outside Anthropic can verify is whether Anthropic's competitors apply comparable caps — and Gemini 4 Carbon, sitting second on the new index without a full public release or comparable public statements, is now the most interesting open question in AI governance. A cap is a competitive sacrifice only if rivals do not secretly decline to make it.

⚠️ Engineering takeaway

The caps do not change what you can build with Fable 5.2 — they change what you can assume about the roadmap. If your planning model assumes each generation's model can accelerate the next generation's training (kernel-level and architecture-level self-improvement), Anthropic has published, in the system card, that its own loop is deliberately not running at full speed. Budget accordingly: the capability curve you are riding is the capped one, and the uncapped capability — wherever it exists — is behind someone else's verification wall.

06 — The edge organizes: LFM3-Harness

October 27 gave the edge the models (Liquid AI's LFM3 family — six 2B-class open-weight models on one tri-hybrid backbone, ~1 GB at 4-bit); November 1–2 gave the edge the pattern. LFM3-Harness is an independent, MIT-licensed community project — explicitly not affiliated with Liquid AI — published as v0.1.1 with a project site and GitHub repository, and it is the first complete wiring of Liquid's family into a supervised local agent stack: a d1-2B decision model as router, an LFM3-1.7B actor, LFM3-VL-2B for vision and LFM3-Audio-2B for voice, all pinned to CPU llama.cpp servers bound to 127.0.0.1 — no cloud calls anywhere in the loop.

The supervision machinery is the part worth stealing even if you never run the stack. A 75%-floor preflight write gate suppresses approval cards while it holds; frozen SHA-256 acceptance contracts define what “done” means before the work starts; finish claims are refused until the file actually exists on disk; hints are separated from authority so a prompt-injection hint cannot grant permissions; and allow-once approvals expire. The project's own README is honest about the unfinished parts — and its own benchmark run caught the router silently faking itself, the exact failure mode the harness exists to catch. That last fact is the best single argument for the whole approach: the defect was found by the acceptance machinery, not by a user.

This site covered the project in three pieces the week it shipped — a source-level deep dive (November 1), a setup guide (November 2) covering the hash check, the 4.9 GB weight fetch and the one mission that has passed end-to-end acceptance, and seven verification patterns portable to your own agent. The strategic reading: October's dispatch argued the frontier's most consequential competition had moved to the edge, where the units are gigabytes and milliseconds. November's evidence is that the edge community agrees — and is building the trust machinery the big labs sell as products, on a GPU-less laptop, in two weeks, from models that cost about a gigabyte each.

📌 Reading this section honestly

LFM3-Harness is an introduction to machinery, not an endorsement of capability. The project's own materials are explicit that parts are unfinished and the benchmark record is one mission deep. Frequently Asked Questions

What is Claude Fable 5.2?

Claude Fable 5.2 is Anthropic's frontier release of November 9, 2026 — the generally available half of a one-model-two-regimes structure the company has used since June. It carries a 1M-token context, 128K output tokens, adaptive thinking that is always on (controlled through the effort parameter, which now defaults to high), a June 2026 knowledge cutoff, and pricing of $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output — the Fable line's first-ever cut, down from 5.1's $10/$50. It debuts at No. 1 on Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index v5 with a reading of 65 — the highest reading ever published on any version of the index — and it is the same underlying model as the restricted Claude Mythos 5.2, with production safeguards layered over sensitive domains. What is Claude Mythos 5.2 and who can use it?

Mythos 5.2 is the unrestricted twin of Fable 5.2 — same weights, same 1M context, same $8/$40 pricing — reserved for organizations verified through Anthropic's programs, most relevantly the Cyber Verification Program for security workloads and the life-sciences program for bio. Anthropic describes it as its most capable model for cybersecurity engineering: it finds 10.2% more vulnerabilities than Opus 5.5 and 14.4% more than Mythos 5.1, led at every compute tier in Anthropic's testing, and one in six of its confirmed finds was missed by every other frontier model tested. General-access users interact with the Fable variant, whose guardrails block most security-offensive task categories outright.

Are Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 the same model?

Yes — same underlying weights, same 1M-token context, same 128K output ceiling, same $8/$40 pricing, same adaptive-thinking-always-on contract. The difference is the safeguard layer, not the model. One reading caution follows from this: benchmark rows that mix “Fable 5.2” and “Mythos 5.2” results are mixing two safeguard regimes. The cyber headline numbers are the known case — The Hacker News attributes 76.3% on CyScenarioBench and 89.6% on CVE Rediscovery to Fable 5.2, The Decoder attributes both to Mythos 5.2, and this dispatch carries the disagreement rather than resolving it.

How much does Fable 5.2 cost?

$8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens through the API — down from Fable 5.1's $10/$50, double Opus 5.5's $4/$20, and slightly under GPT-6.1's roughly $10/$50. Cache writes run $10 (5-minute) and $16 (1-hour) per million tokens, cache reads $0.20, and the Batch API takes a flat 50% off input and output ($4/$20). Artificial Analysis measured an average task cost of roughly $26.54 at max reasoning settings — the most expensive model in its comparison set, about 2.6× Opus 5.5's ~$10 — though the model burns fewer tokens per task than its predecessors (~98,000 output tokens per task at Max), and extra-high effort captures most of max's quality at a fraction of the cost. On claude.ai, access starts at the Pro tier — it is not on the free tier.

Did Kimi K3.1 Pro's weights ship on November 4?

Yes. Moonshot published the full model weights on November 4, 2026, exactly on the schedule it set on October 23, under an Apache 2.0 licence: 2.8 trillion total parameters with 104 billion active (Stable LatentMoE), Kimi Delta Attention, MXFP4-quantized experts, a 1M-token context, and the disclosed cyber controls that held xdev-bench-hard at 81.0% against an uncontrolled checkpoint's 90.5%. What changed in the Claude API contract with Fable 5.2?

Four things to audit before you flip traffic. First, thinking-block prefix binding is now enforced for every account — Fable 5.1 only hard-failed for accounts created on or after August 31, 2026 — so stacks that edit conversation history (injecting reminders, reordering turns, mutating tool results) start returning 400s. Second, the effort parameter now defaults to high across surfaces, which silently raises cost per turn for teams that relied on medium defaults. Third, the standing Fable-line rules carry over: no temperature, top_p or top_k (omit the fields), no forced tool choice (use auto with strict schemas). Fourth, refusals are not HTTP errors — the model returns HTTP 200 with stop_reason: "refusal" and a stop_details object, unbilled when nothing was emitted; treat refusal as a routing event, not an exception.

What exactly did Anthropic cap in Fable 5.2?

Two domains, both named: accelerator kernel optimization and improving large language model architectures. Anthropic says it deliberately throttled capability in those areas during training rather than letting the model reach its full potential there, because unrestricted capability in either would directly accelerate AI development itself. The scoping matters: agentic coding, hardware development and traditional AI research — including interpretability — are uncapped, and the release's demos (the 1,000-agent OS build, the 30-hour Fermat formalization, the three working devices) all proceed at full capability. Whether Anthropic's competitors apply comparable caps — Gemini 4 Carbon's silence is the live case — is the open governance question this release leaves behind.

Should I switch from Opus 5.5 to Fable 5.2 for everyday coding?

Anthropic's own guidance says no, and the independent data agrees. Opus 5.5 remains the recommended daily driver at half the per-token cost, Sonnet 5.5 actually posted the better knowledge-work Elo (1,813 versus 1,785 on AA-Briefcase), Gemini 4 Carbon leads the business-workflow boards, and independent testers still preferred Opus 5.5's visual output even when Fable's mechanics were faster. Reach for Fable 5.2 on the hard 10%: long-horizon agentic engineering, formal science and mathematics, hardware design, and orchestration over fleets of cheaper models — the advisor pattern, where Fable plans and reviews while Haiku- or Sonnet-class models execute, routes around the cost problem without giving up the capability.

What is LFM3-Harness?

An independent, MIT-licensed community project published November 1–2, 2026 — not affiliated with Liquid AI — that wires four of Liquid's LFM3 models into a supervised local agent stack: a d1-2B decision-model router, an LFM3-1.7B actor, LFM3-VL-2B for vision and LFM3-Audio-2B for voice, all pinned to CPU llama.cpp servers on 127.0.0.1. Its supervision machinery includes a 75%-floor preflight write gate, frozen SHA-256 acceptance contracts, finish claims refused until the file exists on disk, hints separated from authority, and expiring allow-once approvals. Its own benchmark run caught its router silently faking itself — the failure mode the harness exists to catch. Our deep dive, setup guide and verification patterns cover it in full.
